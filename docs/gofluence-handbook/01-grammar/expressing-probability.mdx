# Expressing probability

:::info Assumptions
This page is about the **past and present**. For the future, see the [expressing possibility](./expressing-possibility.mdx) page. The class notes use `probable` for `may, might, could`; here it means "maybe true, maybe not".
:::

## 1. Learning objective

Use modals to say how sure you are that something **was** or **is** true: certain, probable or impossible.

## 2. Key concepts

Choose the modal that matches how sure you are.

| Certainty | Modal | Present | Past |
|-----------|-------|---------|------|
| Sure it is true | must | You must enjoy traveling. | You must have enjoyed traveling. |
| Probable (maybe) | may / might / could | They may/might/could be busy right now. | They may/might/could have been busy yesterday. |
| Sure it is impossible | can't | She can't be happy about getting fired. | I can't have missed the bus. |

```text title="Structure"
Present: modal + base verb
Past:    modal + have + past participle
```

:::tip Think of a scale
`must` = about 95% sure it is true. `may / might / could` = about 50% sure. `can't` = about 95% sure it is false.
:::

:::note `must` here is not obligation
In this page, `must` means "I am sure". It does not mean a rule. See [must](./must.mdx) for both meanings.
:::

:::warning Use `can't`, not `mustn't`, for impossible
For a negative guess, use `can't` or `can't have`. Do not use `mustn't`.
- Correct: She can't be at work. She is on holiday.
- Wrong: She mustn't be at work.
:::

:::danger Spanish speakers
Spanish *debe de estar* (I am sure) and *puede que esté* (maybe) are different degrees of certainty. In English, use `must` when you are sure and `may / might / could` when you are not. Do not use `can` for a positive guess about the past: say *might have gone*, not *can have gone*.
:::

## 5. Common mistakes

| ❌ Wrong | ✅ Correct | Why |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| You must enjoyed traveling. | You must **have enjoyed** traveling. | Past guess: must + have + past participle. |
| They might busy right now. | They might **be** busy right now. | Use `be` before an adjective. |
| She mustn't be happy about getting fired. | She **can't** be happy about getting fired. | `can't` expresses "impossible". |
| I can't missed the bus. | I can't **have missed** the bus. | Past: can't + have + past participle. |
| They may been busy yesterday. | They may **have been** busy yesterday. | Do not forget `have`. |

## 6. Practice exercises

### A. Fill in the blanks

1. The lights are on. Someone ______ (must / be) in the office.
2. I do not know where Sam is. He ______ (might / be) at lunch.
3. The server crashed. A developer ______ (must / change) the settings.
4. She just got fired. She ______ (can't / be) happy.

### B. Rewrite the sentence

Rewrite each sentence with the modal in brackets.

1. I am sure that the client enjoyed the demo. (must)
2. Maybe the team was busy yesterday. (might)
3. It is impossible that I missed the call. (can't)

### C. Speaking prompts

- A colleague is late. Make **three guesses** about why with `must`, `might` and `can't`.
- Your website is down. Say **what probably happened** in the past using `must have` and `could have`.

## 8. Quick recap

- `Must` = sure it is true. `May / might / could` = maybe. `Can't` = sure it is impossible.
- Present: modal + base verb (must be, might be).
- Past: modal + have + past participle (must have been, might have gone).
- Use `can't`, not `mustn't`, for a negative guess.
