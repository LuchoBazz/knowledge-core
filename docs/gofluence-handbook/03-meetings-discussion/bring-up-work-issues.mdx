# How to bring up work issues

## 1. Learning objective

Bring up work issues in a clear and polite way by **expressing observations, sharing new information and giving reminders**, using the **present perfect** when needed.

## 2. Key concepts

When you bring up a work issue, you usually do one of three things.

| Purpose | Example |
|---------|---------|
| Express an observation | I've noticed that our printer hasn't been working properly. |
| Share new information | I wanted to let you know the company has a new overtime policy. · I think you should know that our latest product isn't doing so well. |
| Give a reminder | I'd like to remind you that using Facebook at work is a major offense. |

### Present perfect for issues

The present perfect connects a past event with the **present**. It is useful when the problem started before now and still matters.

```text title="Structure"
have/has + past participle
It has come to my attention that ...
I have noticed that ...
```

:::tip Soft openings
`I've noticed that…`, `I wanted to let you know…` and `I think you should know that…` prepare the other person. They sound better than starting directly with the problem.
:::

:::note Contractions
In speaking, use contractions: `I've noticed`, `hasn't been working`. In a formal email, `It has come to my attention that…` is also common.
:::

:::warning Say the facts, not the blame
Describe what you **see**: `The report has not arrived.` Avoid `You never send the report.` Facts are easier to accept.
:::

## 5. Common mistakes

| ❌ Wrong | ✅ Correct | Why |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| I noticed that the printer doesn't work since Monday. | I've noticed that the printer **hasn't worked** since Monday. | `since` needs the present perfect. |
| It has come to my attention that... (to a close friend) | I've noticed that... | The first phrase is formal. Match the situation. |
| I have noticed that the printer have not worked. | I have noticed that the printer **has not** worked. | Third person singular: `has`. |
| I'd like to remember you that... | I'd like to **remind** you that... | `remind` = recordar a otra persona. `remember` = recordar algo. |
| I think you should to know that... | I think you should **know** that... | Modal + base verb, no `to`. |

:::danger Spanish speakers
*Recordar* has two meanings. In English: **remind** someone (*Te recuerdo que…* = `I'd like to remind you that…`) and **remember** something yourself (*Recuerdo que…* = `I remember that…`). Do not say *I'd like to remember you*.
:::

## 6. Practice exercises

### A. Fill in the blanks

1. I ______ (notice) that the Wi-Fi has been slow all week.
2. I'd like to ______ you that the deadline is Friday.
3. It ______ come to my attention that the report is late.
4. The printer ______ (not / work) properly since Tuesday.

### B. Rewrite the sentence

Rewrite each sentence with a softer opening from the lesson.

1. You never update the ticket.
2. The new overtime policy starts on Monday.

### C. Speaking prompts

- Tell a colleague about a problem in the office. Use `I've noticed that…`.
- Share new information with your team using `I wanted to let you know…`.
- Give a polite reminder about a deadline or a rule.

## 8. Quick recap

- Three purposes: express observations, share new information, give reminders.
- Use soft openings: `I've noticed that…`, `I wanted to let you know…`, `I'd like to remind you that…`.
- Use the present perfect for problems that started before now.
- `remind` someone, `remember` something.
- State facts, not blame.
